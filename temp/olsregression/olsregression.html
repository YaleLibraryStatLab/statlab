Abstract

This research guide provides a comprehensive introduction to ordinary least squares regression (OLS), including the theoretical and mathematical background of OLS, the assumptions of OLS, when to use OLS in practice, how to implement OLS regression, and how to interpret results from OLS regression.

Keywords

OLS, Regression, Continuous Variables, Categorical Variables, R, lm, t.test

Introduction

Regression is a powerful body of statistical methods for constructing equations to describe the relationship between a response variable (often referred to as a dependent or outcome variable) and a set of predictor variables (often referred to as independent variables). Regression models can be used to achieve 4 statistical goals (Montgomery, Peck, and Vining 2021):

Goal Immediate target What else is needed
Description The least-squares fit for the observed data Correct units, coding, support, and a clear descriptive estimand
Population inference A population CEF or best linear projection A defensible sampling design or an explicit superpopulation/model-based framework
Prediction Outcomes for new units from a named target process Prediction uncertainty, common support, and out-of-sample validation
Causal inference A counterfactual contrast such as the ATE, ATT, or CATE An identification strategy: consistency, exchangeability/randomization, positivity, and an appropriate specification

The focus of this guide will be on one type of regression: ordinary least squares regression (OLS).

Identification Comes First

The table above makes an important distinction explicit: fitting a model and identifying a quantity of interest are two different tasks. lm() — or any other regression command — will return a numeric answer no matter how the data were collected. Whether that answer is meaningful depends entirely on decisions made before the model is ever fit.

Identification is King

Regression does not create a research design. Regression is an estimator — or, in some cases, an approximation — applied to data only after the identifying variation needed for the quantity of interest has already been defended. If that variation has not been defended, no amount of model complexity, additional covariates, or robust standard errors can manufacture it after the fact (Angrist and Pischke 2009; Blackwell 2013).

This distinction matters most for causal claims. It is tempting to assume that a random sample is enough to justify a causal interpretation, but random sampling and causal identification answer two different questions:

  • Random sampling — drawing observations at random from a population — supports design-based generalization: the ability to extend an estimate from the sample to the broader population it was drawn from.

  • Causal identification requires something else: random assignment of treatment, a natural experiment, or a defensible conditional-independence (“unconfoundedness”) argument. Any of these can justify treating an association as causal.

As such, a random sample does not, on its own, make a regression causal, and a nonrandom sample does not necessarily rule out causal inference — what matters is how the treatment or exposure came to vary, not how the sample came to be observed.

This is also why it is useful to distinguish two quantities that are easy to conflate. The first is the observed conditional expectation function (CEF):

\[E[Y \mid D = d, X = x]\]

which is purely descriptive — the average of \(Y\) among the units we happen to observe with treatment \(D = d\) and covariates \(X = x\). The second is the causal response surface:

\[E[Y(d) \mid X = x]\]

which is defined in terms of a potential outcome, \(Y(d)\): the average \(Y\) we would see if every unit in the \(X = x\) stratum were set to \(D = d\), including units we actually observed at other values of \(D\). Regression, by itself, only ever estimates the first quantity.

Connecting the observed CEF to the causal response surface — that is, licensing a causal reading of a regression coefficient — requires several additional conditions: consistency (treatment is well-defined and there is no interference between units), conditional exchangeability or randomization (treatment is as-good-as-random within levels of \(X\)), and positivity/overlap (every level of \(X\) has some chance of appearing at every level of \(D\)). Angrist and Pischke (2009) describe the result of these conditions as a “causal CEF”: regression can estimate a causal conditional comparison, but only once the research design — not the regression itself — has identified that comparison. See Blackwell (2013) for a fuller treatment of the causal-CEF, binary-treatment, and heterogeneous-effects case.

These conditions are not abstract. Consider two regressions that look superficially similar:

  • math ~ read + income, which we will fit on our CASchools data later, is only an adjusted association. Neither reading score nor income was assigned to a district — both simply co-occur with math score — so the coefficient on read estimates the observed CEF, not a causal response surface, no matter how many additional covariates we add to the model.

  • In a randomized binary-treatment study, outcome ~ treatment estimates the difference in mean outcomes between the treatment and control groups. Because treatment was randomly assigned, exchangeability holds by design, and this difference-in-means is a causal effect — the treatment-control difference is the ATE. Randomization supplies the causal warrant; OLS just performs the calculation.

Even when identification holds, a regression coefficient is rarely the whole argument on its own. As Spirling and Stewart (2025) argue, regression is one piece of a broader evidentiary case — the identification strategy, the measurement, and the substantive theory all have to hold together for a coefficient to support the claim being made. For an example of how specific designs deliver identification, see our Instrumental Variables Guide.

Fitting the Causal Model

Once identification holds, fitting the model is often the easy part — but the right tool depends on the design that delivered identification. In a randomized experiment, a simple lm(outcome ~ treatment) recovers the ATE directly. Other designs need different estimators built on the same OLS machinery: instrumental-variables designs use two-stage least squares (e.g., ivreg() from the AER package), difference-in-differences designs use panel/fixed-effects models, and regression discontinuity designs use local linear regression near the cutoff.

We return to these ideas below as we assess the research design behind our own example.

Assessing the Research Design

Imagine that you work for the California’s State Department of Education. You have been sent to visit a randomly selected school district in the state. What would you expect the average math score to be in this district? How can you leverage other data about these districts to help with your predictions?

Before we begin to fit our statistical models, we should first consider the scientific question of interest and assess the research design of our study.

  1. Our scientific question of interest is how district-average math scores for California primary school districts might relate to other aspects of these districts, like their district-average reading scores, grade spans, and district-average income.

  2. So, one of our estimands would be the change in our response variable (district-average math score) given a one-unit change in one of our continuous predictor variables (while holding all other variables constant). Similarly, another estimand could be the difference in our response variable between different levels of a factor variable (while holding all other variables constant). These are associational estimands, not causal effects — a distinction we’ll return to below.

  3. Our unit of analysis is each California primary school district, and many of our variables are district-averages.

Be Careful with Ecological Inference

Our data is at the district-level, so our associations cannot automatically be interpreted as student-level or school-level associations. This, instead, becomes an issue of ecological inference: the process of extracting information about individuals from information reported at the group or aggregate level (King, Rosen, and Tanner 2004).

  1. Our observations are not a sample in the usual design-based sense — the data are a census of all California K–6 and K–8 school districts with available testing data for the 1998–99 school year. As such, there is no target population here in the way that phrase usually implies: there is no larger group of California districts from which these 420 were drawn and to which we could generalize. This has two consequences. First, even our descriptive claims should not be extended beyond the census’s scope (e.g., to high school districts in California, primary school districts in other states, or districts observed in other years). Second, any population-level p-values or confidence intervals we report later can only be justified under an explicit superpopulation or model-based framework — treating these 420 districts as one realization of a hypothetical repeated-sampling process — rather than under a defensible probability-sampling design. See Blackwell’s chapters on design-based and model-based inference for a fuller treatment of this distinction (Blackwell 2026).

  2. None of the claims we make will be causal, since there were no interventions or randomization in this study, and we will not be leveraging any identification strategies to make causal claims.

  3. Because none of our claims are causal, our estimands are identified by cross-district variation rather than by an assignment mechanism. Because this variation is purely observational — districts were not assigned their income or reading levels — any association we find could still reflect confounding (e.g., income driving both reading and math scores) rather than a direct relationship between reading and math performance. The regression coefficients describe how math scores co-vary with the predictors across districts, not what would happen if a district’s reading score were experimentally changed.

Specifying and Fitting a Linear Model

There are an infinite number of lines that can be “fit” to a scatterplot of two variables.

How do we know what line best fits the data? Different methods differ in how they choose the “best” line for the data.

In OLS, this is done by minimizing the sum of squared residuals (SSR). A residual is the difference between the observed dependent variable, \(Y\), and the fitted or predicted values of the dependent variable, \(\hat{Y}\) (see Blackwell (2026), chapter 7, for a full derivation of the least-squares objective).

For OLS with one predictor, we obtain fitted values using the regression equation \(\hat{Y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + X\hat{\beta}_1\), where \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) are the estimated regression coefficients. So, in this setting, the OLS estimates \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) are defined as the values of \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) that minimize the sum of squared residuals:

\[(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1) = \arg\min_{\beta_0, \beta_1} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \beta_0 - X_i\beta_1)^2\]

For OLS with multiple predictors, we obtain fitted values using the regression equation \(\hat{Y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + X_1\hat{\beta}_1 + X_2\hat{\beta}_2 + ... + X_k\hat{\beta}_k\), where \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1, \hat{\beta}_2, ..., \hat{\beta}_k\) are the estimated regression coefficients. So, in this setting, the OLS estimator \(\hat{\beta}\) is defined as the value of \(\beta\) that minimizes the sum of squared residuals:

\[\hat{\beta} = \arg\min_{\beta} \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^\top \beta)^2\]

What Does OLS Actually Estimate?

The \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) that appear in the population model \(Y = \beta_0 + X\beta_1 + \varepsilon\) are not something we observe directly — they are the coefficients of the population best linear projection (BLP) of \(Y\) onto \(X\): the linear function that minimizes population mean-squared error,

\[(\beta_0, \beta_1) = \arg\min_{b_0,\, b_1} \, E\big[(Y - b_0 - Xb_1)^2\big].\]

OLS is the sample analogue of this population problem: it replaces the population expectation with a sample average and finds the \(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1\) that minimize the sum of squared residuals in the data we actually observe (as in the formulas above). \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) are estimators of \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) — not the population quantities themselves.

It’s also worth being precise about which population quantity OLS is targeting. The conditional expectation function (CEF), \(E[Y \mid X = x]\), is the true average of \(Y\) at each value of \(X\), and it can take any shape, linear or not. The best linear projection is, by construction, always linear. If the CEF happens to be linear in \(X\), the two coincide, and \(\beta_0, \beta_1\) describe the CEF exactly. If the CEF is not linear, OLS still estimates the best linear projection — the line that comes closest to the CEF under squared-error loss — but that line is only an approximation, and it may not answer the substantive question a researcher actually cares about (see Blackwell (2026), chapter 6, for a fuller treatment of the CEF and best linear projection).

This also clarifies the difference between an error and a residual, two terms that are easy to conflate. The population error (or projection error) is \(\varepsilon = Y - \beta_0 - X\beta_1\): the gap between \(Y\) and the population BLP, evaluated at the true, unobserved coefficients. We never observe \(\varepsilon\) directly. The sample residual is \(e = Y - \hat{\beta}_0 - X\hat{\beta}_1\): the gap between \(Y\) and the fitted line, evaluated at the estimated coefficients. Residuals are observable, and they are what we actually plot and compute when checking assumptions later in this guide — but they are not the same object as the population errors those assumptions are stated in terms of.

What Does “Linear” Mean, Exactly?

Before fitting anything, we need to be precise about what “linear” refers to, since specifying a model means choosing a functional form, and that choice is easy to get wrong in a specific way. Linearity does not mean that \(Y\) must vary with \(X\) along a line — it means the model must be linear in its unknown parameters. For example,

\[Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \beta_2 X^2 + \varepsilon\]

is still a linear regression, even though the fitted relationship is curved in \(X\): the unknown coefficients \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2\) each enter the equation linearly (multiplied by a known function of \(X\) and summed). By contrast,

\[Y = \beta_0 + \exp(\beta_1 X) + \varepsilon\]

is nonlinear in the parameter \(\beta_1\), since \(\beta_1\) sits inside a nonlinear function of itself — this can’t be fit with OLS and requires nonlinear least squares instead.

This distinction can trip up reparameterizations that look harmless. A term like \(\beta_2^2 X\) is nonlinear in \(\beta_2\), even though it’s linear in \(X\). It might be tempting to relabel \(\gamma = \beta_2^2\) and fit \(Y = \beta_0 + \gamma X + \varepsilon\) instead — but \(\gamma\) is a different parameter from \(\beta_2\): because \(\gamma = \beta_2^2 \geq 0\) by construction, this reparameterization silently imposes \(\gamma \geq 0\) and discards any information about the sign of \(\beta_2\).

The linearity assumption that actually matters for OLS, then, is that the conditional expectation function (CEF) is linear in the included regressors — \(E[Y \mid X] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \dots + \beta_k X_k\) for known functions \(X_1, \dots, X_k\) of the original variables, which can include polynomial terms, interactions, or other transformations, as long as the coefficients enter linearly. If the true CEF is not linear in the included regressors, OLS still estimates a best linear projection (see What Does OLS Actually Estimate? above), but that projection may not equal the CEF (see Blackwell (2026), chapter 6, on nonlinear functions of covariates). We come back to how this is checked, and what to do if it fails, in Diagnostics and Influence below.

How Well Does the Model Fit?

The equal-means model from earlier is exactly the intercept-only model: its only “prediction” is the sample mean, \(\bar{Y}\), for every district. We can quantify how much better our fitted line does using the total and residual sums of squares.

The total sum of squares (SST) measures how much the observed math scores vary around their mean — the squared-error loss of the intercept-only model:

\[SST = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})^2\]

The sum of squared residuals (SSE) measures how much is left over after fitting the line — the squared-error loss of our fitted model:

\[SSE = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\]

\(R^2\) compares the two:

\[R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}\]

\(R^2\) is often over-interpreted:

  • \(R^2\) cannot decrease when more predictors are added to a model, even irrelevant ones — OLS can always set an added predictor’s coefficient to (approximately) zero, so adding predictors can only reduce or leave unchanged the in-sample SSE. This makes \(R^2\) a poor tool for comparing models with different numbers of predictors.
  • A high \(R^2\) does not establish causality: it measures how much of the variation in \(Y\) is associated with \(X\) in this sample, not whether that association reflects a causal effect (see Identification Comes First above).
  • A high \(R^2\) does not establish calibration or correct specification: a model can fit the observed data well and still be a poor description of the true CEF, or produce systematically biased predictions in some region of \(X\).
  • A high in-sample \(R^2\) does not establish out-of-sample performance — it says nothing about how well the model predicts data it was not fit on (see the held-out comparison later in this guide).

Adjusted \(R^2\) is a limited correction for the first problem: it penalizes \(R^2\) for the number of predictors in the model, so it can decrease when an added predictor does not improve the fit enough to be “worth” the extra parameter. Our model’s adjusted \(R^2\) is about 0.851 — nearly identical to \(R^2\) here, since we have only one predictor. But adjusted \(R^2\) is still an in-sample quantity, computed on the same data used to fit the model; it is not a substitute for validating a model’s performance on data it has not seen.

Quantifying Uncertainty

OLS and the Gauss–Markov Theorem

OLS Is BLUE, Not “Best” in Every Sense

Under a set of classical conditions (below), the Gauss–Markov theorem says OLS is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) of \(\beta\) (Blackwell 2026, chap. 8). “Best” has a narrow meaning here: among all estimators that are (a) linear functions of the observed outcomes and (b) unbiased for the same coefficient vector, OLS has the minimum variance. Lower variance matters because it means our estimates are more precise from sample to sample — tighter confidence intervals, more power to detect real effects, less risk that any one estimate is far off.

But “best” does not mean the best research design, the best predictor, the best causal estimate, or the best estimator among every possible estimator. Biased estimators (ridge, lasso) can have lower total error than OLS; nonlinear estimators can outperform OLS when the truth is nonlinear; and no amount of statistical efficiency compensates for a design that hasn’t identified the quantity of interest.

The theorem requires three conditions: (1) a correctly specified linear conditional mean / zero conditional error, \(E[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0\); (2) full column rank (no predictor is an exact linear combination of the others); and (3) homoskedastic, uncorrelated errors. Normality is not required — it is only needed for exact small-sample \(t\)- and \(F\)-tests; large-sample inference is still available without it.

Two consequences follow. An unbiased estimate of the wrong estimand is not helpful: if condition (1) fails, OLS is unbiased for the wrong thing (the best linear approximation, not the true CEF). And under heteroskedasticity, OLS point estimates typically remain consistent, but the textbook efficiency result no longer holds and the conventional standard-error formula is wrong — which is exactly why we check for it in Diagnostics and Influence below.

Coefficients, Standard Errors, and What They Answer

Before turning to statistical significance, summary() reports several distinct quantities, each answering a different question (see Blackwell (2026), chapter 8, for the formal statistical theory behind them):

  • The coefficient estimate (\(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.861\)) is our point estimate of the population parameter — what is our best guess for the size and direction of the relationship?
  • The standard error (SE) estimates how much \(\hat{\beta}_1\) would vary from sample to sample if we repeated this study on a new set of districts — how precisely is this estimate pinned down?
  • The 95% confidence interval is (approximately) \(\hat{\beta}_1 \pm 1.96 \times SE\) — what range of effect sizes is consistent with the data?
  • The test statistic (t-value) is the coefficient divided by its SE — how many standard errors is the estimate from the null value (usually 0)?
  • The p-value is the probability of observing a test statistic at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were exactly true — how surprising is this data, if there were truly no linear relationship?

Below, we walk through how to assess and address the conditions most relevant to our regression examples: linearity, homoskedasticity, no autocorrelation, and (for models with multiple predictors) linear independence.

Linearity

We defined what “linear” means for a model like this — and why it matters — in What Does “Linear” Mean, Exactly? above. Here, we check it, and cover what to do if it fails.

Assessing and Addressing Violations

We can get an initial sense of whether the CEF looks linear in the included regressors by creating a scatterplot of the predictor and outcome variables. From the scatterplots of math and reading scores above, the relationship between these two variables seems to be linear, so the linearity assumption seems to hold for model_math_read.

Transformations and basis expansions (polynomials, splines) do not automatically repair a linearity violation — they change what the model is capable of fitting, but the resulting specification still needs to be checked, and it now targets a different, transformed estimand. A few points are worth being deliberate about:

Transforming the outcome changes the estimand, not just the scale. A common fix for curvature is to log the outcome, the predictor, or both. Each specification answers a different substantive question:

Specification Model Interpretation of \(\beta_1\)
Level–level \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon\) A one-unit increase in \(X\) is associated with a \(\beta_1\)-unit change in \(Y\)
Log(Y)–level(X) \(\log(Y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon\) A one-unit increase in \(X\) is associated with an approximate \(100\beta_1\%\) change in \(Y\) (exact: \(100(e^{\beta_1}-1)\%\))
Level(Y)–log(X) \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(X) + \varepsilon\) A 1% increase in \(X\) is associated with an approximate \(\beta_1/100\)-unit change in \(Y\)
Log–log \(\log(Y) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(X) + \varepsilon\) A 1% increase in \(X\) is associated with an approximate \(\beta_1\%\) change in \(Y\) (an elasticity)

Handling zeros and negative values is not trivial. The log is undefined at zero and below, which matters whenever \(X\) or \(Y\) can be zero or negative (income, test-score differences, count data). A common workaround is to add an arbitrary constant, like \(\log(Y+1)\) — but the choice of “+1” is arbitrary (it could just as easily be \(+0.5\) or \(+10\)), and the resulting coefficient’s meaning depends on that choice, particularly when many observations are near zero. If zeros are common, it is usually better to use a transformation designed for that setting (e.g., the inverse hyperbolic sine) or a model suited to the outcome’s distribution (see GLMs, below), rather than patch the log.

Approximate interpretations become misleading for large coefficients. The “\(100\beta_1\%\)” reading of a log-outcome coefficient is a small-\(\beta_1\) approximation; the exact one-unit effect on the original scale is \(100(e^{\beta_1}-1)\%\), which diverges from the approximation as \(|\beta_1|\) grows.

Retransformation is not automatic. If the goal is to predict \(Y\) itself (not \(\log Y\)) after fitting a log-outcome model, naively exponentiating the fitted values is biased, because

\[E[Y \mid X] \neq \exp\big(E[\log(Y) \mid X]\big)\]

without an additional correction (e.g., Duan’s smearing estimator). Jensen’s inequality guarantees these two quantities differ whenever \(Y\) has any spread on the log scale.

Beyond transformations, curvature can also be addressed by changing the functional form itself, and different tools address different problems:

  • Polynomials add terms like \(X^2\), \(X^3\) to capture smooth curvature while remaining linear in the parameters (see above) — simple, but can behave poorly at the boundaries of the data or under extrapolation.
  • Splines join low-order polynomials smoothly at a set of knots, offering more local flexibility than a single global polynomial while remaining linear in the parameters (and so still fittable with OLS on an expanded set of basis functions).
  • Generalized additive models (GAMs) let the CEF be a sum of flexible, data-driven smooth functions of each predictor, without committing to a functional form in advance — useful when the shape of the relationship is itself unknown.
  • Nonlinear least squares is for models that are nonlinear in the parameters, like \(Y = \beta_0 + \exp(\beta_1 X) + \varepsilon\) above — it requires iterative optimization rather than the closed-form OLS solution.
  • Generalized linear models (GLMs) address a different problem: an outcome whose distribution doesn’t fit OLS’s assumptions (binary outcomes, counts), via a link function relating a linear predictor to the mean of that distribution. A GLM is not a generic fix for curvature in \(X\) — it is a fix for the outcome’s distribution, and the specific link function used introduces its own particular nonlinearity in the mean-predictor relationship on the original scale.

Homoscedasticity of Errors

Homoscedasticity of errors is the assumption that the error terms have equal variance across values of the predictor variable. As such, this assumption could be violated when the variance of the outcome variable changes across values of a predictor variable.

Homoskedasticity is not required for OLS coefficients to exist, or even for them to be consistent — it specifically supports the conventional variance formula and the textbook Gauss–Markov efficiency result (see OLS and the Gauss–Markov Theorem above). When it fails, point estimates typically survive, but the standard errors, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests computed from the default formula do not.

Robust standard errors fix exactly this. Robust (e.g., Huber-White) standard errors recompute the uncertainty around the coefficient estimates without assuming constant error variance (Zeileis, Köll, and Graham 2020). Switching SE estimators changes the standard errors, confidence intervals, and p-values — not the OLS point estimate itself — and they are not a general-purpose fix: they do not repair confounding, nonlinearity, measurement error, or a bad research design. Nor are they automatically “reliable” just because they relax homoskedasticity: they still require the correct dependence structure (a heteroskedasticity-robust SE does not, by itself, account for clustering), an adequate number of observations or clusters, and appropriate finite-sample corrections. See our standard errors guide for a full comparison of conventional, heteroskedasticity-robust, clustered, and serial-correlation-robust choices.

No Autocorrelation of Errors

No autocorrelation of errors is the assumption that errors of different observations are not correlated. This kind of dependence doesn’t come from nowhere — it follows from the data-generating structure of the study: time (observations close together in time), geography (nearby units), repeated observations of the same unit, or districts nested within counties or other clusters.

Assessing and Addressing Violations

We can check this assumption by plotting residuals against a variable that reflects the data’s actual dependence structure (e.g., time for a time series, a grouping variable for clustered units), or with a formal test like the Durbin-Watson test (Hothorn et al. 2024b) or the Breusch-Godfrey test (Mitchell and Zeileis 2025).

Linear Independence of the Predictor Variables

This assumption applies when there is more than one predictor variable (i.e., when fitting a multiple linear regression model). Linear independence of the predictor variables is the assumption that no predictor variable can be expressed as an exact linear combination of the others. Two versions of this problem are often lumped together:

  • Exact collinearity — one predictor is a perfect linear combination of the others (e.g., including both a variable and a constant multiple of it, or a full set of dummy variables plus an intercept) — makes \(\mathbf{X}^T\mathbf{X}\) singular, so a unique OLS solution does not exist at all; lm() will typically drop a variable automatically or return NA for its coefficient (see Blackwell (2026), chapter 7, on the full-rank condition).
  • High collinearity — predictors are strongly but not perfectly correlated — does not prevent estimation and does not by itself bias the coefficients. What it does is inflate their variance: with highly correlated predictors, many different combinations of coefficients fit the data almost equally well, so small changes in the sample can produce large swings in the estimated coefficients, even though the model’s overall fit and predictions remain stable.

When this second, milder problem occurs, it is referred to as multicollinearity.

Assessing and Addressing Violations

Since we only have one predictor variable in model_math_read, this assumption is not relevant to it — but it becomes relevant to the multiple-regression models fit later in this guide. We can check for collinearity by calculating a correlation matrix of all pairwise correlations between predictor variables. A value of exactly 1 or -1 indicates perfect (exact) collinearity; a value close to 1 indicates high collinearity.

We can also calculate the Variance Inflation Factor to determine how much the variance of a regression coefficient is increased due to multicollinearity (Thompson et al. 2017). A high VIF, such as VIF \(>5\), indicates high multicollinearity — but a high VIF is a signal to investigate, not a mechanical trigger for action. Dropping a predictor because its VIF crosses some threshold changes the estimand: the remaining coefficients are no longer estimates of “the effect of \(X_1\) holding \(X_2\) constant,” since \(X_2\) is no longer in the model, and if the dropped variable is a genuine confounder, removing it can introduce omitted-variable bias that is worse than the variance inflation it was meant to fix. A high VIF is better treated as a prompt to ask whether both predictors are needed for the substantive question being asked, or to check whether the imprecise coefficients still support the conclusions the analysis relies on — not as a rule to mechanically drop whichever variable has the highest VIF.

Summarizing the Simple Model

With diagnostics complete, here is where model_math_read stands. The linearity and independence checks raised no concerns, and the model shows a clear positive relationship between reading and math scores: every one-point increase in district-average reading score is associated with an expected 0.861-point increase in district-average math score. The Breusch-Pagan test did find evidence against homoskedasticity, however, so while we can trust the coefficient itself, the default standard errors, t-values, and p-values cannot be trusted as computed — any claim about statistical significance should instead use the HC3-robust or county-clustered SEs computed above, not the defaults from summary(). With that caveat in mind, the fitted equation can still be used to predict a district’s math score from its reading score, though the next sections attach honest uncertainty to that prediction rather than treating it as exact.

Extending the Model

So far, we have only considered how to use OLS regression to model the relationship between a continuous predictor variable (i.e., district-average reading score) and a continuous outcome variable (i.e., district-average math score). However, OLS regression can also be used to model the relationship between a categorical predictor variable and a continuous outcome variable, between an outcome and several predictors at once, and between predictors whose relationship with the outcome depends on each other. This section extends the specification-and-fit stage to each of these in turn, using the same diagnostic and interpretive habits established above.

Multiple Predictors

OLS extends naturally to multiple predictors — continuous, categorical, or both — which is often more realistic than a single predictor. Fitting a multiple regression in R is easy; interpreting it correctly raises two issues.

First, adding a predictor changes what every other coefficient means. The grades coefficient in our simple regression was exactly the difference in group means; with another predictor added, it becomes a conditional comparison — the difference holding the other predictor fixed — a different question (see Covariates and Pressure-Testing an Interaction below).

Second, deciding which predictors to include should not be left to a mechanical search procedure. Stepwise regression (forward, backward, or both) is a common default in introductory treatments, but a poor one: it repeatedly tests many candidate models against the same data, inflating false positives, producing selections unstable to small changes in the data, and invalidating the standard errors and p-values reported for the final model. Pre-register hypotheses and analysis plans instead, and choose predictors using substantive knowledge of their causal role — confounder, mediator, collider, instrument, or precision variable — rather than a mechanical search. See our variable-selection guide for how to reason through that choice.

For comparing a small number of candidate models directly, a partial F-test (or the equivalent nested likelihood-ratio test) tests the joint null that a specific set of added coefficients are all exactly zero, under the same conditions as the rest of this guide’s inference (see OLS and the Gauss–Markov Theorem above) — and only applies to nested models. AIC and BIC compare models, nested or not, by penalizing in-sample fit for parameter count, but stay in-sample. Cross-validation is the only one that estimates out-of-sample performance directly (see the held-out comparison later in this guide). A partial F-test and AIC/BIC ask whether a more complex model fits the observed data better; cross-validation asks whether it predicts new data better.

With those caveats in mind, here are the benefits.

Covariates

Our simple regression found a positive relationship between reading and math scores. This could partly reflect a common cause: districts with higher reading scores also tend to have higher average income, and income is plausibly related to math scores through educational resources and support. If income is a confounder — a common cause of both — the simple read coefficient partly reflects income’s influence, not reading and math alone. Whether income (or any variable) belongs in the model depends on its causal role: confounders, mediators, colliders, instruments, and precision variables each change the resulting coefficient differently, and not always for the better (see our variable-selection guide, and Blackwell (2026), chapter 6, for the formal omitted-variable-bias result). How Uncertain Is This Prediction?

Before trusting a single predicted value like \(692.103\), consider how much uncertainty surrounds it. Two related quantities help:

\[MSE = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \frac{SSE}{n}, \qquad RMSE = \sqrt{MSE}\]

RMSE is more interpretable than MSE since it’s back on the outcome’s original units — here, math-test points.

# mean squared error and root mean squared error of the fitted model
MSE <- SSE / nrow(CASchools)
RMSE <- sqrt(MSE)
MSE
[1] 52.01936
RMSE
[1] 7.212445

Our model’s RMSE is about 7.2 points. This is training error — how far off predictions are, on average, for the same data used to fit the model — and it’s usually optimistic, since the coefficients were chosen to minimize error on this data. A more honest estimate comes from held-out data, or cross-validation (see below).

There’s also a second kind of uncertainty. Predicting \(692.103\) for a district with a reading score of 700 could mean two things, and predict() distinguishes between them:

# uncertainty about the AVERAGE math score among districts with read = 700
predict(model_math_read, newdata, interval = "confidence")
       fit      lwr      upr
1 692.1025 690.4002 693.8049
# uncertainty about a SINGLE NEW district's math score
predict(model_math_read, newdata, interval = "prediction")
       fit      lwr      upr
1 692.1025 677.7899 706.4152

The confidence interval (\([690.4, 693.8]\)) is uncertainty about the mean math score among districts with a reading score of 700 — it only has to account for how precisely we’ve estimated \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\). The prediction interval (\([677.8, 706.4]\)) is uncertainty about one new district’s score — it adds the individual variability around the line (\(\varepsilon\)) that more data can’t shrink away. That’s why the prediction interval is substantially wider: predicting one new observation is harder than estimating an average, even holding the model fixed.

Both intervals assume we’re predicting within the observed range of reading scores — roughly 605 to 704 (see the discussion of support in What Does OLS Actually Estimate? above). A reading score of, say, 400 or 900 would be extrapolation: predict() will still return a number, with no warning that it’s left the region the model was estimated from.

None of this — including the \(R^2\) from earlier — tells us how the model performs on districts it hasn’t seen. To check that, we hold out part of the data, fit on the rest, and compare predictions on the held-out portion to a baseline: the intercept-only model.

# 80/20 train-test split, fitting the simple and intercept-only models on the training set
set.seed(42)
n <- nrow(CASchools)
train_idx <- sample(seq_len(n), size = floor(0.8 * n))
train <- CASchools[train_idx, ]
test <- CASchools[-train_idx, ]

fit_train <- lm(math ~ read, data = train)
intercept_only_train <- lm(math ~ 1, data = train)

pred_fit <- predict(fit_train, newdata = test)
pred_intercept_only <- predict(intercept_only_train, newdata = test)

# root mean squared error on the held-out data
rmse_fit <- sqrt(mean((test$math - pred_fit)^2))
rmse_intercept_only <- sqrt(mean((test$math - pred_intercept_only)^2))

# mean absolute error on the held-out data
mae_fit <- mean(abs(test$math - pred_fit))
mae_intercept_only <- mean(abs(test$math - pred_intercept_only))

rmse_fit
[1] 6.651132
rmse_intercept_only
[1] 18.06438
mae_fit
[1] 5.338602
mae_intercept_only
[1] 15.00357

The fitted model’s held-out RMSE (about 6.65) is substantially lower than the intercept-only model’s (about 18.06), and the same holds for MAE (5.34 vs. 15.00). This — not the in-sample \(R^2\), and not one fitted point from the training data — is the real evidence that read improves predictions for districts the model wasn’t fit on.

Sensitivity and Reporting

Before reporting results from any OLS model, it helps to work through a short checklist. Applying it here to our final model, model_math_read_grades_interaction (math ~ read * grades):

  1. Estimand: The (non-causal) association between district-average reading score and district-average math score, and whether that association differs by district grade span — see Assessing the Research Design.
  2. Unit of analysis: Each California K–6 or K–8 school district.
  3. Analysis sample: All 420 districts in the CASchools census; no missing data in any variable used, so every model in this guide is fit on the same sample (see the FAQ below).
  4. Specification: math ~ read * grades, a continuous predictor interacted with a binary categorical predictor, chosen to test whether the reading-math relationship differs by grade span, building on the simpler models fit earlier in this guide.
  5. Coefficient estimates and CIs: For K-8 districts (the reference group), the estimated intercept is 97.040 and the slope on read is 0.849; for K-6 districts, the intercept is 68.996 and the slope is 0.894 (see Pressure-Testing an Interaction). As with the simpler models earlier in this guide, confidence intervals should be reported alongside these.
  6. SE choice: We have not tested this model’s homoskedasticity assumption directly. Given the heteroskedasticity we found in the simple model, robust standard errors would be a safer default for this model’s SEs, CIs, and p-values too.
  7. Fit: Not reported for this model in this guide; as discussed in How Well Does the Model Fit?, any \(R^2\) here would still only describe in-sample fit.
  8. Diagnostics and influence: Not re-run for this specific model in this guide (recall that assumption checks do not carry over automatically from the simple model); the leverage, studentized residual, and Cook’s distance checks in the FAQ below illustrate how you would do this.
  9. Prediction uncertainty or causal limitations: None of the coefficients in this guide support a causal claim (see Identification Comes First) — read and grades were not assigned to districts, so every coefficient here describes an adjusted association among the districts we observe, not the effect of changing a district’s reading score or grade span.
  10. Sensitivity analysis: Not conducted for this specific model in this guide; the leave-one-out check in the FAQ below, applied to the simple model, found the results were not driven by a single district — a similar check on this final model would be needed before relying on it.

Not every applied analysis will have a clean answer for all ten items — but working through the list, and being honest when an item has not been checked, is more useful than an unexamined summary() output.

FAQ

Can I use OLS when the outcome is binary?

Mechanically, lm() will work even if \(Y\) is binary. When \(Y \in \{0, 1\}\), the conditional expectation function is a probability:

\[E[Y \mid X] = P(Y = 1 \mid X)\]

so fitting \(Y\) on \(X\) with OLS estimates a linear probability model (LPM) — a linear approximation to how \(P(Y=1)\) varies with \(X\). Coefficients are read in probability points: \(\hat{\beta}_1 = 0.05\) means a one-unit increase in \(X\) is associated with a 5-percentage-point increase in \(P(Y=1)\) — not a 5% increase, and not log-odds (as in logit).

When the predictor is also binary — a treatment indicator \(D \in \{0,1\}\) — the LPM is exact: as with grades earlier, the intercept is \(P(Y=1 \mid D=0)\) and the slope is the difference in proportions, \(P(Y=1\mid D=1) - P(Y=1\mid D=0)\). lm(Y ~ D) and a two-proportion test do the same arithmetic.

Whether that difference is causal depends on where \(D\) came from, not on using OLS. A randomized \(D\) gives exchangeability by design and a causal coefficient — the same logic as the randomized outcome ~ treatment example in Identification Comes First. An unrandomized \(D\) gives only an adjusted association.

What are the limitations of the linear probability model?

Even when identification is not the concern, the LPM has three structural problems:

  • Inherent heteroskedasticity. A Bernoulli outcome has variance \(p(X)(1-p(X))\) by construction, which depends on \(X\) — so homoskedasticity fails by the model’s structure, not just empirically. Robust SEs (see OLS and the Gauss–Markov Theorem) are close to mandatory here.
  • Fitted values outside \([0,1]\). A straight line can predict probabilities below 0 or above 1 — values with no valid probability interpretation.
  • Constant marginal effects. The LPM assumes a one-unit change in \(X\) moves \(P(Y=1)\) by the same amount regardless of the starting probability — but a bounded probability should show shrinking effects near 0 or 1. Logit and probit build that in; the LPM doesn’t.

For binary outcomes where these limitations matter, see our logit and probit guide for the alternatives and how to fit and interpret them.

Can I use OLS for counts, proportions, or ordinal outcomes?

Mechanically, yes — lm() fits a line to any numeric outcome: counts, proportions, ordinal scales. Whether it’s a good choice is separate, and often the answer is no:

  • Squared-error loss treats all errors as equally costly regardless of scale — a poor match for skewed or bounded outcomes.
  • Predictions outside the outcome’s support are possible for the same reason as the LPM above — a fitted count can be negative, a fitted proportion can exceed 1.
  • Skew and dispersion are common in count data especially (many zeros, a long right tail), which OLS does not model.
  • Ordinal scoring is arbitrary. Treating a 1–5 scale as evenly spaced assumes the gap between 1 and 2 means the same as the gap between 4 and 5 — an assumption the data may not support.

Outcome-specific models — Poisson or negative binomial regression for counts, beta regression for proportions, ordinal logistic regression for ordinal scales — are usually a better starting point. See our logit and probit guide for the binary case.

What does a multiple-regression coefficient actually represent?

The Frisch–Waugh–Lovell (FWL) theorem describes exactly what a multiple-regression coefficient is. For the coefficient on \(X_1\) with additional regressors \(X_2\):

  1. Regress \(Y\) on \(X_2\) (and the intercept) and keep the residuals, \(\tilde{Y}\).
  2. Regress \(X_1\) on \(X_2\) (and the intercept) and keep the residuals, \(\tilde{X}_1\).
  3. Regress \(\tilde{Y}\) on \(\tilde{X}_1\) — the resulting slope is exactly \(\hat{\beta}_1\) from the original multiple regression.

This is often called “partialling out” or “residualizing”: the multiple-regression coefficient on \(X_1\) is the association between whatever variation in \(Y\) and \(X_1\) is not explainable by \(X_2\).

FWL is arithmetic, not causal inference. It explains what number the coefficient is, not that including \(X_2\) achieved causal control. Whether residualizing on \(X_2\) moves us closer to a causal quantity still depends entirely on \(X_2\)’s role — confounder, mediator, collider, instrument, or precision variable each change what the residualized coefficient represents (see our variable-selection guide).

How do I check for influential observations?

Not every observation contributes equally to a fitted regression line. A few diagnostics quantify this:

  • Leverage (\(h_{ii}\), the diagonal of the hat matrix) measures how unusual an observation’s predictor values are, independent of its outcome — high-leverage points can pull the fitted line toward themselves.
  • Studentized residuals rescale residuals by their estimated standard deviation (excluding that observation’s own influence), making them comparable across observations.
  • Cook’s distance combines both — how much the fitted values for the whole model would change if an observation were dropped — capturing points influential through high leverage and a large residual (see Blackwell (2026), chapter 7, for formal definitions).
# leverage, studentized residuals, and Cook's distance for each observation
lev <- hatvalues(model_math_read)
stud <- rstudent(model_math_read)
cooksd <- cooks.distance(model_math_read)

# the 5 observations with the largest Cook's distance
top5 <- order(cooksd, decreasing = TRUE)[1:5]
data.frame(row = top5, leverage = lev[top5],
           studentized_resid = stud[top5], cooksD = cooksd[top5])
    row    leverage studentized_resid     cooksD

416 416 0.014832789          2.073782 0.03212132
417 417 0.016570365          1.952888 0.03191536

Rules of thumb exist (leverage above \(2(k+1)/n\), Cook’s distance above \(4/n\)), but they’re flags for investigation, not verdicts. In our simple model, several districts exceed the leverage rule of thumb and a handful have Cook’s distance modestly above \(4/n\) — but none approach the classic threshold of 1, or stand out in magnitude.

The most direct way to check whether a flagged observation actually matters is a leave-one-out sensitivity check: refit the model without it, and see how much the coefficients move.

# read coefficient with and without the most influential district
worst <- top5[1]
model_math_read_loo <- lm(math ~ read, data = CASchools[-worst, ])

coef(model_math_read)
(Intercept)        read 
 89.5657974   0.8607668 
coef(model_math_read_loo)
(Intercept)        read 
 92.1111770   0.8568171

Dropping the single most influential district changes the read coefficient from about 0.861 to about 0.857 — a negligible shift. This is reassuring: our earlier interpretations do not hinge on any one district.

If a leave-one-out check did reveal a large change, the right response isn’t automatic deletion — that can bias the analysis toward what the analyst wanted, and discards real information. Investigate instead: is this a data quality issue (a coding error, an implausible value)? Is it scientifically relevant, or a fundamentally different kind of unit? Does the model have adequate support at its predictor values, or is it extrapolating? Whatever the answer, report the sensitivity — readers should know a result depends heavily on one or two observations, whether or not those observations are excluded.

How does lm() handle missing data?

By default, lm() silently drops any row with a missing value in a used variable — complete-case analysis — and R won’t warn you. This matters for two reasons: if missingness relates systematically to the outcome or predictors, complete-case analysis can bias results, not just shrink the sample; and different specifications using different variables can silently end up fit on different subsets of rows, making their coefficients, fit statistics, and any nested-model comparisons (like the partial F-tests discussed above) hard to compare, since they no longer describe the same districts.

Report the analysis sample and check missingness explicitly, rather than relying on the default:

          0           0           0
